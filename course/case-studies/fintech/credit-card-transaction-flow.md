A credit card transaction's defining property is that a real-time approve-or-decline decision on someone's money must complete in a couple of seconds across four independently-operated parties who each speak the same decades-old message format — while the actual money never moves until a slower batch runs a day or two later. That split is the whole story. The tap you feel at the terminal is an **authorization**: a request that hops merchant to acquirer to card network to issuer, gets a yes or no, and places a hold on the cardholder's available credit. The money itself moves during **clearing and settlement**, a batched process that runs on a T+1 to T+2 cycle, nets each issuer against each acquirer, and pays the merchant the transaction amount minus fees. Most explanations blur these two into one; a system designed on card rails has to keep them rigorously separate, because they have completely different latency budgets, failure modes, and correctness requirements.

This case study designs a system with the shape of the card networks (Visa and Mastercard as the canonical examples), grounded in the **four-party model**, the **ISO 8583** message format, the **EMV** chip cryptogram, and the interchange economics that decide who pays whom. The four parties are the **cardholder**, the **merchant**, the merchant's bank (the **acquirer**), and the cardholder's bank (the **issuer**), with the **card network** sitting in the middle as the interchange switch that routes messages and later nets settlement.

## Step 1: Outline use cases and constraints

### Use cases

#### We'll scope the problem to handle the following use cases

* A **cardholder** taps or inserts an **EMV chip card** at a **merchant** terminal, and the terminal builds an **authorization request** describing the purchase (amount, currency, merchant, how the card was presented) and asks whether it should be allowed
* The request routes merchant to **acquirer** to **card network** to **issuer**, the issuer approves or declines in real time, and the response returns along the same path to the terminal within roughly two seconds — the cardholder hears the beep and leaves
* On approval, the issuer places a **hold** on the cardholder's available credit for the authorized amount; no money has actually moved yet
* Later, the merchant **captures** the authorized amount, the acquirer submits **clearing** records to the network, and the network runs **settlement** on a T+1 to T+2 batch cycle, netting positions and moving funds so the merchant is paid the amount minus **interchange** and scheme and acquirer fees
* The system carries an **EMV cryptogram** (the ARQC) generated by the chip so the issuer can cryptographically verify the card was genuinely present, not cloned
* When the issuer is temporarily unreachable, the network can perform **stand-in processing** (STIP) against issuer-supplied rules rather than failing every transaction
* Every message is expressed in **ISO 8583**, so any acquirer, network, and issuer in the world can parse it

#### Out of scope

* The card network's fraud and risk scoring models in detail — treated as a real-time decision service the issuer (or stand-in) calls, not designed here
* Cardholder onboarding, credit underwriting, and how an issuer sets a credit limit
* Chargebacks, disputes, and the full representment lifecycle — named in the talking points, not fully designed
* Tokenization and network token provisioning (the mechanism behind mobile wallets) — mentioned as a real extension
* Three-party (closed-loop) schemes such as American Express operating as its own issuer and acquirer — this design uses the four-party open-loop model
* PIN and cardholder verification method selection details beyond noting where the cryptogram and PIN are validated

### Constraints and assumptions

#### State assumptions

* The authorization decision is **real time** with an end-to-end budget of about **two seconds** at the terminal; the network itself typically applies a timeout on the order of a few seconds before it considers the issuer non-responsive and either stands in or declines
* Money movement is **not** real time: authorization only places a hold, and the actual funds move during **clearing and settlement** on a deferred **T+1 to T+2** batch cycle
* The vast majority of transactions use the **dual-message** model: a separate authorization message at tap time and a separate clearing message later, rather than one combined message
* Amounts are handled as **integer minor units** (cents), never floating point, so netting and fee math are exact
* The system must remain available at extreme scale — a card network processes on the order of **hundreds of billions of transactions per year**, so no single node can sit on the path of every authorization
* The **EMV cryptogram** must be verifiable by the issuer, and the raw PAN and sensitive authentication data fall under **PCI-DSS** scope wherever they are stored or transmitted
* The network routes to the correct issuer using the card's **BIN** (bank identification number, the leading digits of the PAN)

#### Calculate usage

* Annual volume: a large network processes on the order of **200+ billion transactions per year**. Taking roughly 200,000,000,000 / 31,500,000 seconds per year gives about **~6,300 authorizations/sec average**. Card traffic is sharply peaked around holidays and daily commerce hours, so design for well over **10x average at peak**, on the order of **tens of thousands of authorizations/sec**.
* Latency budget: the two-second wall clock the cardholder experiences is spent mostly in two places — the two network hops (acquirer to network, network to issuer) and the issuer's own decision (validity, available credit, fraud scoring). A practical split is roughly a few hundred milliseconds of network transit plus a few hundred milliseconds of issuer decision, leaving comfortable headroom under the network's multi-second timeout.
* Authorization message size: an ISO 8583 authorization request with an EMV data field is on the order of **~300 to 700 bytes** depending on how many data elements and how large field 55 (EMV data) is. At tens of thousands per second, a network region moves single-digit to low-tens of megabytes per second of authorization traffic in each direction.
* Clearing volume: every approved authorization eventually produces a clearing record, so the nightly batch processes on the order of the **same hundreds of billions per year**, but off the hot path and amortized over batch windows.
* Settlement postings: instead of moving funds once per transaction, the network **nets** each issuer against each acquirer per settlement cycle, so the number of actual fund movements per cycle is on the order of the number of **issuer-acquirer pairs**, not the number of transactions — orders of magnitude fewer money movements than authorizations.
* Storage: an authorization plus its later clearing record, keyed by network reference and retaining the ISO 8583 fields needed for reconciliation and disputes, runs on the order of **~1 KB per transaction**; at hundreds of billions per year this is a multi-petabyte-per-year archive that must tier to cold storage while staying queryable for the dispute window.

## Step 2: Create a high-level design

![Four-party card model showing a cardholder tapping an EMV chip card at a merchant terminal, the authorization request (ISO 8583 0100 with the ARQC cryptogram) flowing right through acquirer, card network, and issuer, the response (0110) flowing back, and a separate clearing and settlement band below on a T+1 to T+2 batch cycle](/img/case-studies/fintech/credit-card-transaction-flow-overview.svg)

The core is a **four-party model** with the card network as the switch in the middle. When the cardholder taps, the merchant's **terminal** reads the chip, and the EMV chip computes an **ARQC** (authorization request cryptogram) over the transaction data using a key only the card and the issuer share. The terminal packages the purchase into an **ISO 8583 authorization request** — an MTI 0100 message — carrying the PAN (or a token), amount, merchant category and identifiers, how the card was presented, and the cryptogram in field 55. That message goes to the **acquirer** (the merchant's bank or its processor), which forwards it to the **card network**. The network reads the card's **BIN** to determine which **issuer** to route to, and forwards the request there.

The **issuer** makes the decision: is the card active and not reported lost or stolen, is the ARQC valid (proving the real chip was present), is there enough available credit, and does the transaction pass fraud scoring? If everything passes, the issuer places a **hold** on the cardholder's available credit and returns an **authorization response** — an MTI 0110 — with a response code of `00` (approved) and an authorization code. If not, it returns a decline with a reason code. The response travels back issuer to network to acquirer to terminal, the cardholder sees approved or declined, and the whole round trip fits inside the two-second budget.

Two structural facts separate this from a real-time push-payment rail. First, **approval is not payment.** The 0110 response only places a hold; no money has moved. Second, the money moves in an entirely separate, slower flow: **clearing and settlement.** The merchant later **captures** the authorized amount (batching the day's captures), the acquirer submits clearing records, and the network runs settlement on a **T+1 to T+2** cycle. During settlement the network **nets** what each issuer owes against what each acquirer is owed and moves the net funds through a settlement bank. The **interchange fee** flows from the acquirer to the issuer, the network takes its **scheme fee**, the acquirer keeps its markup, and the merchant is paid the transaction amount minus all of it. The design below makes the fast authorization leg correct and bounded in time, while letting the slow settlement leg run as an independent, reconciled batch.

## Step 3: Design core components

### Use case: The terminal builds an ISO 8583 authorization request

ISO 8583 is the lingua franca of card messaging: a binary format where a fixed **MTI** (message type indicator) names the message, a **bitmap** declares which numbered **data elements** are present, and the data elements follow in order. Because every acquirer, network, and issuer agreed on the field numbering, a message built by a terminal in one country is parseable by an issuer's mainframe on another continent. The authorization request is MTI **0100** and its response is MTI **0110**.

**Core spec: an ISO 8583 authorization message field map and a bitmap builder**

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass

# A small slice of the ISO 8583 authorization field map. Real messages
# carry dozens of data elements (DEs); these are the load-bearing ones
# for a card authorization. Field numbers are per the standard.
AUTH_FIELD_MAP = {
    2:   "Primary Account Number (PAN)",       # the card number, or a token
    3:   "Processing Code",                     # e.g. 00 = purchase
    4:   "Amount, Transaction (minor units)",   # integer cents, right-justified
    7:   "Transmission Date and Time (MMDDhhmmss)",
    11:  "System Trace Audit Number (STAN)",    # per-message trace id
    12:  "Local Transaction Time",
    14:  "Card Expiration Date (YYMM)",
    18:  "Merchant Category Code (MCC)",
    22:  "POS Entry Mode",                      # tap / chip / manual
    37:  "Retrieval Reference Number (RRN)",
    38:  "Authorization Code",                  # present on the 0110 response
    39:  "Response Code",                       # 00 = approved, else decline
    41:  "Card Acceptor Terminal ID",
    42:  "Card Acceptor ID (Merchant ID)",
    43:  "Card Acceptor Name / Location",
    49:  "Transaction Currency Code (ISO 4217)",
    55:  "ICC / EMV Data (TLV, includes the ARQC)",
}

def build_primary_bitmap(present_fields) -> str:
    """Build the 64-bit primary bitmap as a 16-char hex string.

    Bit N (1-indexed from the left) is set when data element N is
    present. Bit 1 is reserved to signal that a secondary bitmap
    (fields 65..128) follows; this builder covers fields 1..64 only.
    """
    bits = 0
    for f in present_fields:
        if not 1 <= f <= 64:
            raise ValueError(f"field {f} needs a secondary bitmap")
        # DE f occupies bit position f, counted from the most significant bit.
        bits |= 1 << (64 - f)
    return f"{bits:016X}"

def build_auth_request(fields: dict) -> dict:
    """Assemble an MTI 0100 authorization request.

    `fields` maps DE number -> already-encoded value. We validate that
    the mandatory elements for an online authorization are present, then
    attach the bitmap that tells the receiver which DEs to expect.
    """
    mandatory = {2, 3, 4, 11, 41, 42, 49}
    missing = mandatory - fields.keys()
    if missing:
        raise ValueError(f"missing mandatory data elements: {sorted(missing)}")

    present = sorted(fields.keys())
    return {
        "mti": "0100",
        "bitmap": build_primary_bitmap(present),
        "data_elements": {f: fields[f] for f in present},
    }
```

**Data structures:** the message is an MTI, a bitmap, and an ordered set of numbered data elements. The **bitmap** is the table of contents — a set bit means "this field is present" — which is what lets a variable set of fields ride in a compact binary frame. Amounts in DE 4 are **integer minor units**, right-justified and zero-padded, never a decimal string.

**Trade-offs:**
* **The gotcha:** ISO 8583 has no single universal schema — the standard fixes the field *numbering* and the MTI and bitmap mechanics, but networks layer their own dialects on top (which private fields, which sub-elements, how field 55 is packed). A message that is valid against Visa's manual can be malformed to Mastercard. The fix is that each network integration is written against **that network's specific ISO 8583 implementation guide**, and messages are validated at the boundary; you never assume one canonical 8583.
* Encoding amounts as integer minor units in DE 4 (rather than a formatted currency string) keeps every downstream sum — the hold, the capture, the netting — exact. A float here would eventually mis-net a settlement batch by a fraction of a cent.

**REST API:**

Terminals speak ISO 8583 over a persistent link, not REST, but an acquirer typically exposes a modern gateway for merchants integrating over the internet. A representative boundary:

### Use case: The chip proves the card is present with an EMV cryptogram

Magnetic-stripe data is static and clonable; the whole point of the **EMV chip** is to make each authorization carry a fresh, unforgeable proof that the genuine card participated. When the terminal runs the transaction, it issues a `GENERATE AC` command to the chip, and the chip computes an **ARQC** (authorization request cryptogram) over transaction data (amount, currency, terminal country, an unpredictable number, and its own counters) using a session key derived from a master key that only the card and the **issuer** hold. The ARQC rides in ISO 8583 **field 55**. Neither the terminal, the acquirer, nor the network can forge it — only the issuer can verify it, which is exactly the trust boundary you want.

**Core spec: how the issuer verifies the ARQC**

```python
import hmac, hashlib

def derive_session_key(master_key: bytes, atc: bytes) -> bytes:
    """Derive a per-transaction session key from the card master key and
    the Application Transaction Counter (ATC). The ATC increments on the
    card every transaction, so each session key -- and thus each ARQC --
    is unique. This is a stand-in for the EMV key-derivation scheme
    (real EMV uses 3DES or AES option A/B); the structure is what matters.
    """
    return hmac.new(master_key, atc, hashlib.sha256).digest()[:16]

def verify_arqc(master_key: bytes, atc: bytes, txn_data: bytes,
                arqc_from_card: bytes) -> bool:
    """Recompute the ARQC over the same transaction data the card signed
    and compare in constant time. A match proves the real chip, holding
    the shared master key, produced this cryptogram for THIS transaction.
    """
    session_key = derive_session_key(master_key, atc)
    expected = hmac.new(session_key, txn_data, hashlib.sha256).digest()[:8]
    return hmac.compare_digest(expected, arqc_from_card)
```

**Data structures:** the issuer holds a per-card **master key** (inside a hardware security module, never in application memory) and tracks the last-seen **ATC** (application transaction counter) per card. Verifying the ARQC recomputes it; checking that the ATC advanced defends against replay of a captured cryptogram.

**Trade-offs:**
* **The gotcha:** the ARQC proves the card was present, but it does not by itself stop a replay if the issuer ignores the counter — an attacker who captured one valid cryptogram could resubmit it. The fix is that the issuer tracks the **ATC** and rejects a cryptogram whose counter is not strictly greater than the last accepted one for that card, so every authorization must carry a fresh cryptogram over fresh data. Real EMV strengthens this further with an unpredictable number contributed by the terminal.
* Keeping the master key and verification inside the issuer's **HSM** means the cryptographic secret lives with the one party already trusted with the account, and keeps the acquirer and network out of scope for that key — the same trust-minimization logic that keeps a UPI PIN at the bank rather than the app.

### Use case: The issuer runs the authorization state machine

Authorization is not a single yes/no — it is a state machine over the life of the money. An approved authorization moves to `AUTHORIZED` and places a **hold**; the merchant later **captures** it (sometimes for less than authorized, as with a tip or a partial shipment), and only a captured authorization becomes eligible for **clearing** and then **settlement**. An authorization that is never captured **expires** and the hold is released. Modeling this explicitly is what keeps holds from lingering and keeps capture amounts from exceeding what was authorized.

**Core spec: the authorization-to-settlement state machine**

```python
from enum import Enum

class AuthState(str, Enum):
    REQUESTED  = "REQUESTED"    # 0100 received, not yet decided
    DECLINED   = "DECLINED"     # issuer said no; terminal state
    AUTHORIZED = "AUTHORIZED"   # approved, hold placed on available credit
    CAPTURED   = "CAPTURED"     # merchant captured; ready to clear
    EXPIRED    = "EXPIRED"      # hold released, never captured; terminal
    CLEARED    = "CLEARED"      # in a clearing batch
    SETTLED    = "SETTLED"      # funds netted and moved; terminal
    REVERSED   = "REVERSED"     # auth undone before capture; terminal

# Allowed transitions: any move not listed here is a bug and must raise.
ALLOWED = {
    AuthState.REQUESTED:  {AuthState.AUTHORIZED, AuthState.DECLINED},
    AuthState.AUTHORIZED: {AuthState.CAPTURED, AuthState.EXPIRED,
                           AuthState.REVERSED},
    AuthState.CAPTURED:   {AuthState.CLEARED},
    AuthState.CLEARED:    {AuthState.SETTLED},
}

def transition(current: AuthState, target: AuthState) -> AuthState:
    """Advance the authorization, refusing any illegal jump. Notably you
    cannot CAPTURE something that was DECLINED, and you cannot SETTLE
    something that never CLEARED -- the money only moves along one path.
    """
    if target not in ALLOWED.get(current, set()):
        raise ValueError(f"illegal transition {current} -> {target}")
    return target

def apply_capture(state: AuthState, authorized_minor: int,
                  capture_minor: int) -> AuthState:
    """Capture may be for at most the authorized amount. Over-capture is
    rejected outright; a lower capture is normal (tips, partial fills).
    """
    if capture_minor > authorized_minor:
        raise ValueError("capture exceeds authorized amount")
    return transition(state, AuthState.CAPTURED)
```

**Data structures:** an `authorizations` row per transaction — `network_reference` (primary key), `pan_token`, `amount_authorized_minor`, `amount_captured_minor`, `currency`, `state`, `auth_code`, `hold_expires_at`, `issuer_bin`, `acquirer_id`, `created_at`, `updated_at`. Amounts are **integer minor units**. The `hold_expires_at` drives an expiry sweep that releases holds for authorizations never captured.

**Trade-offs:**
* **The gotcha:** the hold and the capture are separate events that can diverge — a hold for a restaurant tab may be authorized at the pre-tip amount, then captured higher (with tip) or lower, and an authorization may never be captured at all. Treating the authorized amount as if it were the settled amount over-reports revenue and leaves stale holds squeezing the cardholder's credit. The fix is the **explicit state machine with a bounded hold expiry**: only a `CAPTURED` amount flows into clearing, an uncaptured hold transitions to `EXPIRED` and is released, and capture is validated against the authorized ceiling.
* Making every transition explicit (rather than mutating a status string freely) means an illegal move — capturing a decline, settling something never cleared — raises instead of silently corrupting the money trail. This is the discipline this course's [Idempotency](/docs/patterns/reliability/idempotency) pattern relies on: a replayed capture lands on the same state, not a second one.

### Use case: The network stands in when the issuer is unreachable

The issuer is the authority on the account, but issuers have outages and maintenance windows, and the network's timeout is only a few seconds. Failing every transaction whenever an issuer blips would be unacceptable for a national rail, so the network offers **stand-in processing** (STIP): when the issuer does not respond within the timeout, the network decides on the issuer's behalf using rules the issuer pre-configured (limits, allowed categories, velocity caps). STIP is a graceful-degradation mechanism — the network answers conservatively rather than not at all — and the standing-in authorizations are advised to the issuer once it recovers.

**Core spec: the stand-in / decline decision**

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class StipRules:
    """Issuer-supplied fallback rules the network applies during STIP."""
    stip_enabled: bool
    max_amount_minor: int          # ceiling the network may approve alone
    blocked_mccs: frozenset        # categories never approved in stand-in
    per_card_velocity_cap: int     # max stand-in approvals per card per window

def authorize(req, issuer_client, rules: StipRules, velocity_store):
    """Try the issuer first; fall back to STIP only on timeout/unreachable.

    Returns an ISO 8583-style response code:
      '00' approved, '05' do-not-honor,
      '91' issuer or switch inoperative and stand-in not available.
    """
    try:
        resp = issuer_client.authorize(req, timeout_s=2.0)
        return resp                       # issuer decided; authoritative
    except (IssuerTimeout, IssuerUnavailable):
        pass                              # fall through to stand-in

    if not rules.stip_enabled:
        return AuthResponse(code="91")    # cannot reach issuer, no stand-in

    # Conservative stand-in: only approve what the issuer pre-authorized us to.
    if req.amount_minor > rules.max_amount_minor:
        return AuthResponse(code="05")
    if req.mcc in rules.blocked_mccs:
        return AuthResponse(code="05")
    if velocity_store.count(req.pan_token) >= rules.per_card_velocity_cap:
        return AuthResponse(code="05")

    velocity_store.increment(req.pan_token)
    # Advise the issuer of this stand-in approval once it recovers.
    return AuthResponse(code="00", stand_in=True)
```

**Data structures:** per-issuer `StipRules`, and a `velocity_store` counting stand-in approvals per card within a time window (a fast counter store, since it is on the authorization hot path). Every stand-in approval is queued as an **advice** to replay to the issuer on recovery, so the issuer's ledger converges with what the network approved in its absence.

**Trade-offs:**
* **The gotcha:** stand-in is a liability transfer, not free availability — every approval the network makes without the issuer is a decision the issuer must honor even if that card was actually over-limit or compromised. The fix is that STIP is **deliberately conservative and bounded**: an issuer-set amount ceiling, blocked categories, and a per-card velocity cap keep the network's exposure small, and code `91` is returned rather than a blind approval when stand-in is not applicable. Availability is bought with a bounded, pre-authorized risk budget, not by approving everything.
* Wrapping the issuer call in a timeout and falling back on failure is the [Circuit Breaker](/docs/patterns/reliability/circuit-breaker) and [Timeout](/docs/patterns/reliability/timeout) idea applied per issuer: one struggling issuer must not consume the network's threads or stall unrelated traffic.

### Use case: Clearing and settlement net the money and pay the merchant

Authorization only placed holds; this is where money actually moves. In the **dual-message** model, the merchant **captures** authorized transactions (batching a day's worth), the acquirer submits **clearing** records to the network, and the network runs **settlement**: it computes, for each issuer-acquirer pair, the net of everything the issuer owes for its cardholders' purchases against everything the acquirer is owed for its merchants' sales, and moves only that net through a settlement bank. The fee split is applied here: **interchange** flows acquirer to issuer, the network takes a **scheme fee**, and the merchant receives the transaction amount minus interchange, scheme fees, and the acquirer's markup.

**Core spec: interchange split and multilateral netting in integer minor units**

```python
from collections import defaultdict
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class ClearingRecord:
    acquirer_id: str
    issuer_bin: str
    amount_minor: int              # the captured amount, integer cents
    interchange_minor: int         # acquirer -> issuer, computed per schedule
    scheme_fee_minor: int          # network's cut

def merchant_payout_minor(rec: ClearingRecord, acquirer_markup_minor: int) -> int:
    """What the merchant actually receives on this sale: the transaction
    amount minus the fees deducted along the way. All integer minor units,
    so no rounding can create or destroy money.
    """
    return (rec.amount_minor
            - rec.interchange_minor
            - rec.scheme_fee_minor
            - acquirer_markup_minor)

def net_settlement(records):
    """Multilateral netting: for each (issuer, acquirer) pair, net the
    issuer's obligations against the acquirer's, so the settlement bank
    moves a handful of net figures rather than one transfer per sale.

    Returns net positions in minor units:
      positive  -> the acquirer is owed this by the issuer,
      negative  -> the acquirer owes the issuer (e.g. refunds dominate).
    """
    net = defaultdict(int)
    for r in records:
        # Issuer owes the acquirer the sale amount, less the interchange
        # it earns and the scheme fee that goes to the network.
        net[(r.issuer_bin, r.acquirer_id)] += (
            r.amount_minor - r.interchange_minor - r.scheme_fee_minor
        )
    return dict(net)

def settlement_balances(net):
    """Sanity check: across the whole cycle, the sum of all net positions
    plus the fees withheld must reconcile. This returns the total moved
    per acquirer, which reconciliation compares against captured clearing.
    """
    per_acquirer = defaultdict(int)
    for (_issuer, acquirer), amount in net.items():
        per_acquirer[acquirer] += amount
    return dict(per_acquirer)
```

**Data structures:** a `clearing_records` table keyed by `network_reference`, joined back to the authorization by the same reference so a capture is matched to its hold. A `settlement_positions` table holds the netted `(issuer_bin, acquirer_id, net_minor, cycle_id)` rows the settlement bank acts on. Everything is **integer minor units**, and the interchange amount is computed from the network's published **interchange schedule** (which depends on card type, merchant category, and how the card was presented).

**Trade-offs:**
* **The gotcha:** clearing records must be matched back to their authorizations, but the two are separated by a day or more and can drift — a capture with no matching auth, an auth captured for more than authorized, a settlement figure that does not tie out to the sum of captures. Netting without a **reconciliation** step would quietly settle wrong amounts. The fix is that settlement is paired with reconciliation that matches each clearing record to its authorization by `network_reference`, verifies capture ≤ authorized, and confirms each acquirer's net position equals the sum of its captured clearing minus fees — money that does not tie out is flagged, not silently moved.
* Netting per issuer-acquirer pair (rather than settling every sale gross) is what makes settlement tractable: hundreds of billions of transactions collapse into a small number of net movements per cycle. This is the same decoupling that lets the real-time authorization run without a real-time gross fund transfer per tap.

* **The network switch shards regionally and by BIN range so no single node is on the path of every authorization** — see [Sharding](/docs/patterns/storage/sharding). Routing is a function of the card's BIN, which makes BIN a natural partition key; a request only needs the shard that owns its issuer's route, so the hot authorization path never requires a global coordinator.
* **Every issuer integration gets a [Timeout](/docs/patterns/reliability/timeout) and [Circuit Breaker](/docs/patterns/reliability/circuit-breaker), with [Stand-In Processing](https://www.smartretry.com/glossary/stand-in-processing-stip) as the fallback.** Issuers fail independently, and the network's few-second budget means a slow issuer must be given up on quickly; a breaker that trips on a struggling issuer routes its traffic to STIP under the issuer's pre-set rules instead of holding threads or blindly failing.
* **Isolating each issuer and acquirer integration is a [Bulkhead](/docs/patterns/reliability/bulkhead).** One issuer's outage or one acquirer's traffic spike must not exhaust the switch's shared capacity; bounding each integration's resources keeps a single party's failure from becoming a network-wide event.
* **Authorization is made replay-safe with [Idempotency](/docs/patterns/reliability/idempotency) keyed by the network reference and STAN.** Terminals and acquirers retry on timeout without knowing whether the first attempt reached the issuer; anchoring on a stable per-transaction reference means a retry replays the prior decision rather than placing a second hold.
* **Clearing and settlement run as an independent batch off the hot path** — see [Queue-Based Load Leveling](/docs/patterns/batch-streaming/queue-based-load-leveling). Captures accumulate and are processed on the T+1 to T+2 cycle by the netting engine, decoupling the slow interbank fund movement from the fast real-time authorization the cardholder experiences.
* **A failed leg is undone with a [Compensating Transaction](/docs/patterns/consistency/compensating-transaction), not a distributed lock.** An authorization that must be undone before capture is **reversed** (releasing the hold), rather than holding a two-phase lock across the acquirer, network, and issuer for the duration of the flow — the same reason fast rails prefer compensation over blocking coordination.
* **The authorization and clearing records are an append-only trail** — see [Write-Ahead Log](/docs/patterns/storage/write-ahead-log). Every state transition is durably recorded before being reported, which is what lets reconciliation reconstruct exactly what was authorized versus captured versus settled when a dispute arrives months later.

## Additional talking points

* **Authorization versus clearing and settlement.** The single most-merged pair of concepts on card rails: authorization is a real-time hold decided in about two seconds; clearing and settlement move the money later, batched, on a T+1 to T+2 cycle, and net each issuer against each acquirer. A practitioner must keep them separate in schema, in latency budget, and in failure handling — an approved auth is a promise, not a payment.
* **Interchange economics and who pays whom.** Interchange flows from the acquirer to the issuer and is set by the network's published schedule (varying by card type, merchant category, and presentment). The network earns its scheme fee, the acquirer keeps a markup, and the merchant nets the sale amount minus all of it. The network sets the interchange schedule but does not itself pocket the interchange — a subtlety that surprises people reading a merchant statement for the first time.
* **PCI-DSS scope and tokenization.** The raw PAN and sensitive authentication data are in PCI-DSS scope wherever stored or transmitted, which is why merchants and gateways increasingly handle only **tokens** rather than real card numbers, and why the EMV cryptogram and PIN are validated at the issuer, not held by the merchant. Network tokenization (the mechanism behind mobile wallets) replaces the PAN with a device-bound token so a breach at the merchant does not expose the underlying card.
* **EMV, card-present fraud, and liability.** The chip's ARQC makes card-present cloning cryptographically hard, and the EMV liability shift moved responsibility for counterfeit fraud toward whichever party did not support chip. Card-not-present transactions (online) lack the chip and lean on other controls (address verification, 3-D Secure, risk scoring), which is why online fraud is a different and larger problem than in-person fraud.
* **Stand-in as bounded availability.** STIP keeps the rail up during issuer outages, but every stand-in approval is risk the issuer must honor. Designing it means giving the network a conservative, pre-authorized envelope — amount ceilings, blocked categories, velocity caps — and reconciling stand-in advices back to the issuer on recovery, so availability never quietly becomes unlimited exposure.
* **Reconciliation and disputes.** Because auth and settlement are separated in time, reconciliation is a standing process, not an afterthought: match each capture to its authorization, verify captured ≤ authorized, and tie each acquirer's settlement to the sum of its captures minus fees. Chargebacks and representments ride on top of this trail, which is why the ISO 8583 fields and the network reference must be retained for the full dispute window.
